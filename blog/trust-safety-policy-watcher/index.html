Trust & Safety Policy Watcher: Building Competitive Intelligence That Never Sleeps

How I turned manual competitor policy checks into an automated AI system that watches 25+ Trust & Safety policies and tells product teams what changed.

Lyor ItzhakiJanuary 11, 2025 · 5 min read

The problem: tracking competitors by hand

In social commerce and live streaming, Trust & Safety policies are more than legal pages. When TikTok updates its community guidelines or Meta changes its commerce rules, it can signal where the market is heading, open an advantage, or expose a risk.

Most product teams were tracking these changes by hand, which meant:

  • Hours of manual checking: product managers opening competitor policy pages one by one
  • Late news: learning about a change weeks after it happened
  • Gaps: missing small but important edits
  • No history: no way to see how a policy evolved over time
  • Decisions on stale information: product calls made without the latest competitor context

Teams checked competitor policies maybe once a month, missed most of the meaningful changes, and spent PM hours on routine checking instead of analysis.

I'd spent years in Trust & Safety as a product manager, and this looked like work a machine should do.

The goal: a system that runs on its own

The idea was simple: the best competitive intelligence system works while you sleep. I wanted a "set it and forget it" tool that would be the single source of truth for competitor Trust & Safety policies.

It needed to:

  • Check reliably: visit a curated list of competitor policy pages every 6 hours
  • Tell real changes from noise: flag meaningful updates and ignore cosmetic ones
  • Summarize with AI: write short summaries a product manager can act on
  • Report: send email alerts and keep a dashboard up to date
  • Watch itself: report its own health so people can trust the data

Most competitive intelligence tools need constant upkeep. This one had to run without any.

How I built it

I built it in three phases.

Phase 1: reliable change detection

The first job was fetching pages and spotting changes. The hard part was telling real policy edits apart from cosmetic website updates. Dynamic content, ads, and UI changes caused a lot of false alarms.

I spent weeks figuring out why YouTube policies seemed to change every day when the text was identical. The fix was cleaning the HTML with BeautifulSoup in several layers, so only the core policy text was compared.

Phase 2: AI summaries and alerts

Once change detection was reliable, I added AI analysis. Instead of sending raw diffs, the system asks Google Gemini to answer one question: what changed, and why does it matter for product strategy?

Alerts go out as plain text email. That renders cleanly in every email client, including on phones, where executives often check alerts.

Phase 3: health monitoring

The last phase was about staying reliable. Some sites, like Whatnot and TikTok, block bots, so health checks use both plain HTTP requests and a full browser.

This stopped the system from reporting failures that weren't real, and brought monitoring reliability to 99.9%.

How change detection works

The core of the system is deciding what counts as a change. It cleans each page before comparing it:

  • Find the policy text: use semantic HTML markers to locate the main content
  • Strip the noise: remove ads, scripts, session IDs, and similar elements
  • Filter known patterns: use regex to drop things like nonce attributes
  • Compare twice: check both the HTML structure and the plain text

That way, people only hear about real policy changes, and they keep reading the alerts.

Designing for trust

An intelligence tool is only useful if people trust it. A few choices mattered most:

  • No maintenance: it runs on GitHub Actions and recovers from errors on its own
  • Clean alerts: plain text email that reads well on a phone
  • One dashboard: the policy matrix, history, health status, and exports in one place
  • Summaries about impact: AI summaries focus on what a change means for the business, not on technical detail

The biggest lesson: a tool that monitors competitors has to be more reliable than the competitors it watches. One false alert or a quiet outage and people stop trusting it.

What was hard

1. Bot protection

TikTok, Meta, and Whatnot block ordinary scrapers. I used two fetchers: plain HTTP requests for simple pages and Playwright browser automation for protected ones. That covers every site without slowing the simple ones down.

2. False positives

Early versions reported a change on every run because of timestamps, session IDs, and rotating ads. I built a cleaning pipeline that filters out more than 20 kinds of dynamic content and keeps the actual policy text.

3. Reliability

Teams use this for real decisions, so it can't fail quietly. It has health checks, alerts when something breaks, automatic recovery, and a visible status page. It now runs at 99.9% uptime with no silent failures.

Where it stands

Coverage and performance:

  • 25+ policies across TikTok, YouTube, Meta, Whatnot, and Twitch
  • 99.9% uptime with health monitoring and no silent failures
  • Under 6 hours on average from a policy change to the alert
  • Over 95% precision in telling real policy changes from cosmetic ones

What it saves: the manual checking, an estimated 10+ hours a month per PM, and teams hear about competitor changes while they still matter.

Lessons for product leaders

1. Automation still needs product thinking

Automating the checks wasn't enough. The system needed real product design around how people work, how many alerts they'll tolerate, and how they come to trust it.

Takeaway: internal automation deserves the same product rigor as a customer-facing feature.

2. Use AI for a specific problem

The AI summaries weren't there to show off. They turn raw diffs into something a PM can act on.

Takeaway: add AI where it removes a real pain point, not to demonstrate the technology.

3. Reliability is a feature

For a tool people rely on, uptime is part of the product, not just an engineering concern.

Takeaway: in B2B products, reliability drives trust and adoption.

4. Signal beats volume

A clever system still fails if false alerts teach people to ignore it.

Takeaway: for alerting tools, precision matters more than recall.

What's next

Coverage:

  • Flag competitive openings automatically
  • Connect to strategic planning dashboards
