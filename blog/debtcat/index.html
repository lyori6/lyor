DebtCat: Building a No-BS First Aid Kit for People Drowning in Debt

How a former debt collection product manager built a free tool that helps people understand their rights and dispute unfair collections.

Lyor ItzhakiJanuary 11, 2025 · 5 min read

The problem: debt collection isn't fair

Everyone knows debt is complicated. Fewer people realize the collection system profits when consumers feel confused and ashamed.

Over 70 million Americans have debts in collections. Only a small share know their rights or how to dispute a bad claim:

  • 35% of American adults with credit files have debts in collection
  • 44% of consumers say they feel "scared," "confused," or "helpless" when a collector contacts them
  • Only 1 in 20 consumers disputes a debt, yet up to 59% of debt portfolios contain errors
  • The average consumer spends 45+ hours trying to resolve debt issues

Talk to these people and a pattern shows up. They aren't trying to dodge real obligations. They want clarity, fairness, and a way to challenge mistakes.

Lyor had worked inside debt collection. He knew how the system works and where consumers could push back. Very few products helped them do it.

The idea: insider knowledge, no subscription

"When I worked in collections, I saw firsthand how powerless consumers feel," says Lyor. "People in my community started asking me for help with the system. That's when I thought: what if everyone had the insider knowledge to deal with debt collectors?"

The idea became a "first aid kit for consumers in debt." Plain explanations and practical tools, with no expensive subscription or counseling plan.

Unlike most fintech products aimed at debt, DebtCat doesn't:

  • Require a sign-up or store personal information
  • Push a subscription
  • Give general advice without next steps
  • Focus on debt consolidation (which often just moves debt around)

Instead, it does three things:

  1. Education: clear, jargon-free guides to consumer rights
  2. Conversation: an AI chatbot that answers specific questions
  3. Action: a dispute letter generator that writes legally sound letters

Three tech stacks

DebtCat moved through three tech stacks as its needs changed. Each move was a product decision as much as a technical one.

"I started with WordPress and Elementor because it was the fastest way to get content online," says Lyor. "But as soon as we brought on a designer, we needed more design freedom."

Phase 1: WordPress and Elementor

Worked: fast launch, familiar CMS. Didn't: limited design, slow pages.

Phase 2: Webflow

Worked: much better design control, visual editing. Didn't: cost grew with the content, hard to extend.

Phase 3: Firebase

Worked: scales, cheap, full developer control. Didn't: lost the CMS, components had to be rebuilt.

"Moving from Webflow to Firebase was about money," says Lyor. "We were paying for features we didn't need yet, and I wanted that budget for getting users."

Building solo with AI

DebtCat is also a story about how AI tools let a non-developer build and ship a real web app.

"A few years ago, DebtCat would have needed a full development team," says Lyor. "With AI coding assistants like ChatGPT, Cursor, and Windsurf, I could build it much faster myself."

Each feature followed the same steps:

  1. Plan: define the feature and user flow the usual PM way
  2. Draft components: have AI generate the base HTML and CSS
  3. Wire it up: use AI to connect components and add behavior
  4. Debug: use AI to find and fix problems

"The AI didn't replace product thinking," Lyor says. "But it lowered the technical barrier. I could focus on what we should build instead of getting stuck on how to write the code."

Designing for trust

People bring sensitive financial details to DebtCat. It had to feel safe and take little effort:

  • No account: anyone can generate a dispute letter without signing up or leaving data behind
  • Small steps: information comes in short pieces so it isn't overwhelming
  • Clear pricing: only the mailing services cost money
  • Education first: a library of guides earns trust before asking anyone to act

"Our biggest design insight was that people needed to feel safe before they'd engage," says Dalma, the product designer. "We removed as much friction as we could, even if it meant dropping some nice-to-have features."

What was hard

1. Standing out from the scams

Debt relief is full of predatory services. DebtCat had to show it was different.

"We had to get past the skepticism people have about debt products," says Lyor. "That shaped everything, from our messaging to refusing to ask for payment details upfront."

2. Getting the word out

"Email providers were wary of us because our content mentioned 'debt'," says Lyor. "Google ads had the same problem. We never expected that helping consumers would trip the same alarms as predatory lending."

So the team leaned on social media and community work instead of the usual growth tactics.

3. Technical debt

"When we moved from Webflow to Firebase, a lot of styling and CMS articles broke," says Lyor. "I spent a weekend rebuilding headers, footers, and article pages, knowing I was taking on technical debt to move faster."

Lessons for product leaders

1. Insider knowledge reveals products

Working in collections showed Lyor both the problem and the fix. Market research rarely gets you that.

Takeaway: look for problems your own background lets you see that others miss.

2. Let product needs pick the stack

Moving between three platforms was a product call each time, not a technical preference.

Takeaway: change technical direction when the product needs it, even if it hurts for a while.

3. AI changes who can build

DebtCat got built with almost no development budget. AI tools made that possible.

Takeaway: AI lets product people build more on their own, as long as they know exactly what they want.

4. Trust before revenue

Putting trust ahead of early revenue gave DebtCat a footing in a skeptical market.

Takeaway: in sensitive areas, trust is worth some short-term revenue.

What's next

  • Negotiation tools: help people negotiate directly with collectors
  • Credit reports: pull a credit report to see every debt in one place
  • More guides: content for specific debt situations
  • Trusted partners: work with reputable consolidation and credit counseling services

The team is also considering turning DebtCat into a nonprofit to reach more people.

"The debt collection industry won't change overnight," says Lyor. "But with the right tools and knowledge, people can level the playing field one dispute at a time."

Try it

DebtCat is at debtcat.com, and the core features are free with no sign-up. If you or someone you know is dealing with debt, it's worth a look.
